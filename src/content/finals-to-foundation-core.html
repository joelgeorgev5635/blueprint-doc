Introduction

Before We Start

You have spent five years learning medicine. You know the pathophysiology of heart failure, the mechanism of metformin, the embryological basis of a patent ductus arteriosus. None of that is what this guide is about.

This guide is about the gap — the space between what you know and what you actually need to do at 7am on a ward with nineteen patients, a bleep that hasn't stopped since handover, and a nurse asking you to chart analgesia for a patient you've never met.

The gap is real, it is wide, and no one warned you about it. This guide does.

What follows is what a good registrar would tell you on your first day if they had the time — and none of them do.

What this guide covers
  • How to read and navigate guidelines at clinical speed
  • A prescribing system that catches errors before they reach the patient
  • SBAR escalation that gets registrars out of bed
  • A clerking structure you can run in fifteen minutes
  • Handover that protects patients overnight
  • On-call triage and the sick patient at night
  • The ward culture and social structure no one puts in a curriculum
  • How to recognise burnout early and what to do about it

Chapter 01

Reading Guidelines

Navigating NICE, SIGN, and local trust protocols without losing an hour per query.

It's 9am. A patient has a UTI. You think she needs nitrofurantoin but you're not sure about the dose, whether she's in the right age group, or what the local resistance pattern looks like. You have seven other jobs. You need an answer in under three minutes.

Guidelines are not meant to be read start to finish. They are reference documents. Your job is to navigate them at speed, extract the relevant paragraph, and apply clinical judgment to what you find.

The Hierarchy

Local trust protocol overrides everything else for day-to-day prescribing decisions. Most trusts have antibiotic guidelines, VTE prophylaxis protocols, and insulin management pathways. Find yours in the first week — usually on the trust intranet or a dedicated app. This is the first thing you look up, not NICE.

NICE CKS (cks.nice.org.uk) is your next stop. Short, structured summaries written for clinical use. Each condition has a "Prescribing information" or "Management" section. Go there directly. The "Background" section is for revision, not for 9am on the ward.

Full NICE guidelines are for complex decisions or when CKS doesn't cover your specific question. Use the search function and go to the specific recommendation — not the executive summary.

SIGN guidelines are the Scottish equivalent, particularly useful in specialties where SIGN has produced stronger guidance than NICE.

How to Find the Answer in Under 3 Minutes

  1. Open your local antibiotic/trust guideline first
  2. If it's not there, go to NICE CKS
  3. Search the specific condition or drug, not the broad topic
  4. Read the recommendation — not the evidence base
  5. Check the date: antibiotic guidelines over 3 years old need cross-referencing
Clinical pearl

NICE guidelines describe what should happen in an ideal clinical environment. The ward is not an ideal clinical environment. A guideline that says "consider referral to cardiology for all patients with new AF" does not mean you call the cardiology registrar at 2am for a patient who was in AF three years ago and is medically stable.

Applying a guideline means asking: is my patient in the population the guideline was written for?

Escalate if
  • Local and national guidelines directly contradict each other and you cannot determine which applies
  • You are being asked to deviate from a guideline without a documented clinical reason
  • The guideline is over 5 years old and the decision is material

Chapter 02

Prescribing Safely

The common FY1 prescribing errors, why they happen, and how to stop them before they reach the patient.

It's 11pm. The nurse bleeps you to prescribe analgesia. The patient is 78, has CKD stage 3, and is already on codeine. You're tired. This feels simple. It isn't.

Prescribing errors are the most common source of avoidable harm caused by junior doctors — not from carelessness, but from a system that is complex, scattered, and relentless. You reduce errors not by being more careful, but by having a system.

The Pre-Prescribing Check

Run this every single time, even when it feels excessive:

  1. Allergies. Check the drug chart and EPMA. If the patient has a documented allergy to the class you're prescribing, stop. Verify whether it's a true allergy or intolerance. If unsure, call pharmacy.
  2. Renal function. AKI, CKD stage 3+, or unexpectedly high creatinine requires dose adjustment. Key drugs: NSAIDs (avoid), gentamicin (requires levels), metformin (hold in AKI), DOACs (dose-adjust).
  3. Interactions. EPMA will flag these — read them. The ones that matter: warfarin + almost anything, lithium + NSAIDs/ACEi/diuretics, SSRIs + tramadol (serotonin syndrome risk), QTc-prolonging combinations.
  4. Weight. Required for heparin, gentamicin, and enoxaparin.

The Drugs That Cause the Most Harm

Prescribing rule — Paracetamol
Paracetamol 1g QDS — max 4g/24h
↓ 500mg QDS if weight <50kg
↓ 500mg QDS if hepatic impairment, malnourished, or chronic alcohol use
⚠ Check for paracetamol in combination products (co-codamol, co-dydramol)
High-risk drugs — approach with care
  • Opioids. Never prescribe without knowing the patient's existing opioid load. Opioid-naive adults: start oramorph 2.5–5mg oral prn. Always co-prescribe antiemetic and laxative with regular opioids.
  • Insulin. Never prescribe without knowing current regimen, most recent glucose, and reason for prescribing. If unfamiliar with the regimen, call the diabetic specialist nurse first.
  • Anticoagulants. Confirm indication, duration, and renal function before prescribing. If the patient is on warfarin, check INR before adding anything that interacts.
  • Antibiotics. Follow local protocol. Review at 48–72h when cultures return. Do not continue broad-spectrum antibiotics beyond that window without documented reason.

EPMA Systems

Learn your trust's electronic prescribing system in the first week — before you need it under pressure. The most common errors: prescribing the wrong route (IV instead of oral), the wrong frequency (BD instead of TDS), and failing to complete the "indication" field.

Your best resource: the ward pharmacist

Clinical pharmacists know interactions, dose adjustments, and the local formulary better than you do. This is not an insult — it is their job. If a pharmacist queries your prescription, engage with the query. They are not criticising your prescribing — they are doing their job. Call them before prescribing anything complex rather than after.

When You're Not Sure

Ask pharmacy. If the question is clinical, ask your registrar. If you are being asked to prescribe something outside your competence — insulin regimens, complex anticoagulation, chemotherapy — do not prescribe from memory. Call for help first.

Escalate if
  • A patient has a suspected allergic reaction to a drug you prescribed
  • You discover a significant interaction after prescribing
  • Renal function has deteriorated significantly in a patient on nephrotoxic drugs
  • You are being asked to prescribe something — insulin titration, anticoagulation bridging — that is beyond your current confidence without support

Chapter 03

Escalating Correctly

Confident communication with registrars and consultants. SBAR in practice.

It's 3am. Your patient's NEWS2 has gone from 2 to 7 in four hours. You bleep the registrar. They sound annoyed, ask what you've done so far, and give the impression you're overreacting. You start to doubt yourself. Don't.

Escalation is a clinical skill — not about confidence or seniority. It requires a clear structure and the conviction to use it.

When to Escalate

Mandatory escalation triggers
  • NEWS2 ≥7 at any point
  • Any single parameter scoring 3 (RR, SpO₂, BP, HR, consciousness, temp)
  • Uncontrolled pain not responding to management
  • Acute change in GCS or new confusion in a previously orientated patient
  • Urine output <0.5ml/kg/hr for 2+ hours in a catheterised patient
  • Clinical instinct that the patient is deteriorating and you cannot explain why

Escalate based on trajectory, not just current state. A NEWS2 of 5 rising rapidly over two hours is more concerning than a chronic NEWS2 of 5 at baseline. The trend matters more than the number.

SBAR in Practice

S
Situation
Who you are, which ward, which patient, and exactly what is happening right now. One or two sentences maximum.
"I'm the FY1 on Nightingale ward. I'm calling about Mr Davies, bed 4 — 67-year-old admitted with COPD exacerbation. His NEWS2 has gone from 2 to 7 in four hours and he's increasingly breathless."
B
Background
The relevant clinical context. Not the whole history — what the registrar needs to understand the situation.
"On current treatment of pred and nebs. CXR shows right lower lobe consolidation. He's on aspirin and bisoprolol at baseline, no known allergies."
A
Assessment
Your clinical impression. Say what you think is happening — even if you're not sure.
"I'm concerned he's deteriorating despite initial treatment. RR 28, SpO₂ 88% on 4L. I think he may need escalation to CPAP or ITU review."
R
Recommendation
What you need. Be specific.
"I'd like you to come and review him now. I've ordered an ABG and increased oxygen to 40%."
Before you call

Do what you can before you bleep the registrar. Start oxygen, get IV access, take blood cultures, do an ECG if relevant. The registrar's first question will be "what have you done?" Have an answer. This also means you're treating the patient while you're waiting for them to arrive.

Dealing with Pushback

When a registrar queries your concern or gives advice over the phone rather than coming to review, the most important thing is not to argue — it is to be clinically precise. A registrar who is confident you have done a thorough A-E assessment and can communicate the findings clearly is far more likely to come in than one who receives a vague "I'm a bit worried."

Before you call — do this first

Complete a full A-E assessment. Know the NEWS2, the vital signs trend, and exactly what you found on examination. Have your management so far ready to state. A well-structured SBAR that includes your clinical findings is far more persuasive than a bleep saying the patient "doesn't look right."

The registrar's first question will be "what does the examination show?" Have the answer.

Document all telephone advice — not just conversations where you disagree. This is good practice regardless of outcome. If a registrar gives you management advice over the phone and the patient later deteriorates, clear documentation of the conversation protects both you and the registrar and ensures continuity of care.

Documentation template — all telephone escalations

"Registrar Dr [Name] contacted at 03:15. Patient assessed: A-E findings as above. NEWS2 [score]. Registrar advised [plan]. Will continue to monitor. NEWS2 to be repeated in one hour. Will re-escalate if further deterioration."

If after a thorough clinical assessment and clear communication you remain genuinely concerned and the registrar is not coming to review, document your concern clearly and continue to monitor closely. If the patient deteriorates further, call again. If you believe the patient is in immediate danger and cannot reach your registrar, call the on-call consultant directly.

You are legally responsible for the patients under your care during your shift. Document every clinical decision and every escalation.

Escalate immediately — these cannot wait
  • NEWS2 ≥7 or any single parameter score of 3
  • Cardiac or respiratory arrest
  • Anaphylaxis
  • Suspected sepsis: NEWS2 ≥5 + suspected infection — initiate Sepsis 6 simultaneously
  • Acute stroke symptoms: new focal neurology, FAST positive
  • Unresponsive patient
  • Sudden-onset severe headache ("worst headache of my life")

The Sepsis 6

To be initiated within 1 hour of suspecting sepsis, before or alongside escalation:

Take 3
  • Blood cultures × 2 sets
  • Blood lactate
  • Urine output monitoring (catheterise if needed)
Give 3
  • High-flow oxygen (target SpO₂ ≥94%)
  • IV fluids: 500ml 0.9% NaCl over 15 min
  • IV antibiotics per local protocol

Chapter 04

Clerking Efficiently

Speed and structure without missing the red flags.

You have a new referral from ED. A 71-year-old with two days of worsening shortness of breath. You have fifteen minutes before the ward round starts. The ED clerk says the notes are "in the system". You have never met this patient.

The Structure

Work through this in order. Each section is generating your differential and ruling out red flags in real time — not just producing documentation.

Clerking framework
  • PC — Presenting complaint. One line, patient's words.
  • HPC — Onset, duration, character, severity (1–10), associated symptoms, exacerbating/relieving factors, previous episodes.
  • PMH — Active conditions: IHD, AF, CCF, diabetes, hypertension, CKD, COPD/asthma, liver disease, DVT/PE, malignancy. Surgical history with dates.
  • DHx + Allergies — Every drug, dose, frequency. Use GP letter or EPMA — not patient recall for doses. Document what the allergic reaction was.
  • SHx — Smoking (pack years), alcohol (units/week), occupation if relevant. Living situation, functional baseline: can they walk to the shops? Manage stairs?
  • FHx — First-degree relatives with IHD, malignancy, or heritable conditions relevant to the presentation.
  • ROS — Targeted screen: weight loss, haemoptysis, PR bleeding, haematuria, neurological symptoms, night sweats.
  • Examination — Document positives and negatives. Include vitals and NEWS2 score.
  • Impression and plan — Differential in order of probability. Immediate management, investigations, what would change your approach.
The questions that flag the emergencies
  • "Have you ever had a clot in your leg or your lung?" → DVT/PE history in a dyspnoeic patient
  • "Have you coughed up any blood?" → malignancy, PE, bronchiectasis
  • "Have you lost weight without trying?" → malignancy until proven otherwise
  • "Do you use recreational drugs?" → essential in unexplained cardiac presentations under 50
  • "Has anyone ever told you your heart is abnormal?" → undiagnosed structural disease

System-Specific Red Flags

Respiratory
  • Haemoptysis
  • SpO₂ <94% at rest
  • RR >20 at rest
  • Pleuritic chest pain + risk factors for PE
  • Stridor or use of accessory muscles
Abdominal
  • Peritonism (rigid abdomen)
  • PR bleeding with haemodynamic instability
  • Unintentional weight loss ≥5kg
  • Dysphagia progressive
  • Pulsatile abdominal mass
Cardiac / Neurological
  • Chest pain + diaphoresis + radiation to jaw/arm
  • New focal neurology
  • Sudden-onset severe headache
  • Syncope with exertion
  • Palpitations + pre-syncope
Don't miss on the drug history
  • Immunosuppressants
  • Long-term steroids
  • Anticoagulants
  • Methotrexate
  • Lithium

Writing the Impression and Plan

This is the most important part of the clerking. Write your differential in order of probability, not severity. Then document your immediate plan: investigations requested, treatment started, what you are waiting for, and crucially — what would change your management.

Example impression and plan

"71F presenting with progressive dyspnoea. Most likely: acute exacerbation CCF (bilateral basal crepitations, mild pitting oedema, known CCF on echo 2022). Differentials: pneumonia (fever, right basal creps), PE (new onset, risk factors present).

Plan: CXR, ECG, bloods including BNP and D-dimer, ABG if SpO₂ remains <94%. IV furosemide 40mg if CXR confirms pulmonary oedema. Will reassess after results. If D-dimer elevated and CXR clear → CTPA.

NEWS2 on admission: 4. Increasing monitoring to 2-hourly."

Run every clerking past a senior

As a new FY1, present every admission to your registrar or consultant — even when you feel confident in the plan. This is not a sign of uncertainty; it is good clinical practice and a genuine learning opportunity. Present your differential, your proposed investigations, and your management plan. Let them confirm, correct, or build on it.

The doctors who develop fastest in foundation training are the ones who use every presentation as a teaching moment rather than just a sign-off. A five-minute conversation after a clerking is where you learn to think like a registrar.

Escalate before completing the clerking if
  • Patient becomes acutely unwell during the history
  • NEWS2 ≥5 on initial observations
  • Signs of sepsis, haemorrhage, or airway compromise identified

Chapter 05

Handover That Works

SBAR handover that protects patients and doesn't take 20 minutes.

It's 17:15. You have nine outstanding jobs, two unwell patients, and a night team you've never met arriving in forty-five minutes. The quality of your handover will determine whether your patients are safe overnight.

Bad handover kills patients — not dramatically, not usually. Quietly, through missed jobs, lost information, and a night team left uncertain about what to watch for. Good handover is not long. It is specific.

Written Handover — Per Patient

Handover structure per patient
  • Name, age, bed, admission diagnosis
  • Active issues right now — what is happening, what you have done, what is expected to happen
  • Outstanding jobs — bloods, imaging, referrals, drug charts to review
  • If-then instructions — "If NEWS2 rises above 5, call the registrar; she's aware."
  • What normal looks like for this patient — essential for patients with complex baselines

What to Hand Over

Hand over the sick patients first. Not in bed order — in order of clinical concern. The night team needs to know who to worry about immediately.

Hand over uncertainty. If you think a patient might deteriorate and you're not sure why, say so. "I've not been happy with Mrs Khan all afternoon. I can't find a clear reason. Please keep an eye on her." This is one of the most important things you can say at handover.

What not to hand over
  • Jobs you can do in the next 45 minutes. Do them. Don't hand them over.
  • A differential diagnosis. Give the working diagnosis and management plan.
  • Completed jobs. Only hand over what is outstanding or what the night team needs to act on.

Verbal Handover

For any patient you are genuinely worried about, ask the night doctor to read back the if-then instructions. Verbal information is unreliable. Confirmation that it was received is not bureaucracy — it is patient safety.

Write key instructions in the notes for high-concern patients:

Documentation template — high-concern handover

"FY1 to night team: please review if NEWS2 ≥5. Registrar Dr [Name] is aware and has asked to be called directly on bleep 4421."

Do not leave the ward until
  • Every acutely unwell patient has been verbally handed over to the night doctor directly
  • The night registrar knows about any patient who may need ITU review
  • Outstanding urgent jobs have been communicated and confirmed received

Chapter 06

Nights and On-Calls

Managing pressure, bleeps, and uncertainty at 3am.

It's 2:47am. You have eleven tasks on your list. Your bleep goes off. A patient on the surgical ward has a temperature of 38.4°C and the nurse wants you to prescribe antibiotics. You haven't eaten since 6pm. You've been on since 9am yesterday.

Triage: Your Bleep List

Category 1 — go now
  • Cardiac or respiratory arrest
  • Unresponsive patient
  • Acute haemorrhage
  • Anaphylaxis
  • NEWS2 ≥7 or rapid deterioration
Category 2 — within 20 min
  • NEWS2 4–6, not previously reviewed
  • Glucose <4.0 mmol/L, symptomatic
  • Acute urinary retention
  • Severe uncontrolled pain
  • Acute chest pain
Category 3 — within the hour
  • Routine prescriptions, drug chart queries
  • Non-critical results to review
  • Catheter bypass without systemic signs
  • Sleep sedation requests
Category 4 — morning
  • Non-urgent discharge letters
  • Stable patients with minor queries
  • Routine TTOs
The first question to ask every nurse

When you receive a bleep: "What is their NEWS2?" This tells you immediately which category you're dealing with. Any nurse who cannot tell you should go and check before you decide whether to drop everything.

The Sick Patient at Night

You will be called about patients you've never met, on wards you've never been on, with notes you cannot easily access. This is normal.

  1. Look at the patient first. Before the chart, before the nurse. Do they look well or unwell? Conscious? Breathing? Your eyes are faster than any investigation.
  2. Get the observations. HR, BP, RR, SpO₂, temperature, GCS. Calculate NEWS2.
  3. Get access. If the patient is confused or not responding well, IV access and blood cultures before anything else.
  4. ABCDE assessment. Work systematically.
  5. Treat as you go. Don't wait for a diagnosis to start treatment.
  6. Escalate. If not improving after 15 minutes of treatment, call the registrar.
A — Airway
  • Is it patent? Can the patient speak in full sentences?
  • Look: secretions, vomit, foreign body, tongue position
  • Listen: stridor (partial obstruction), gurgling (secretions), snoring (tongue)
  • Feel: air movement at mouth and nose
  • If compromised: head tilt–chin lift or jaw thrust, suction, consider airway adjunct (NPA/OPA). Call for senior/anaesthetic help immediately — do not delay.
B — Breathing
  • RR: count for a full 60 seconds — the most sensitive early warning parameter
  • SpO₂: on air first, then with supplemental O₂. Target ≥94% (88–92% in known hypercapnic COPD)
  • Work of breathing: accessory muscle use, tracheal tug, intercostal/subcostal recession, nasal flaring
  • Trachea: central or deviated? (deviated away = tension pneumothorax; deviated toward = collapse/fibrosis)
  • Expansion: symmetrical? Asymmetry suggests unilateral pathology
  • Percussion: resonant (normal), dull (consolidation/effusion/haemothorax), hyper-resonant (pneumothorax/emphysema)
  • Auscultation: air entry bilaterally, wheeze (airflow obstruction), fine crackles (pulmonary oedema), coarse crackles (secretions/consolidation), absent breath sounds, pleural rub
  • Intervene: start oxygen if SpO₂ <94% (15L via non-rebreathe mask in acute distress, then titrate)
C — Circulation
  • Heart rate: rate, rhythm (regular/irregular), character (strong, weak, thready)
  • Blood pressure: both arms if concern re: dissection
  • Cap refill: central (<2 sec normal) and peripheral — central delay = shock
  • JVP: raised (heart failure, PE, tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade); flat (hypovolaemia)
  • Skin: pallor, mottling, cyanosis (peripheral or central), diaphoresis, temperature (warm and well-perfused vs. cool and shut down)
  • Heart sounds: I+II, added sounds, murmurs
  • ECG: obtain early in any circulatory concern — rhythm, rate, ischaemia, blocks
  • Urine output: <0.5ml/kg/hr for 2+ hours = oliguria, escalate
  • IV access: two large-bore cannulae in haemodynamically compromised patients. Send bloods: FBC, U&E, LFTs, clotting, CRP, blood cultures if febrile, group & save
D — Disability
  • Consciousness: AVPU (Alert / Voice / Pain / Unresponsive) for rapid assessment; GCS (E+V+M, max 15) for documentation
  • Pupils: size (2–5mm normal), symmetry, direct and consensual light reflex. Unequal or unreactive pupils = urgent neurology concern
  • Blood glucose: always. Hypoglycaemia is immediately reversible and easily fatal if missed. Every acutely unwell patient, no exceptions.
  • Posture: decerebrate or decorticate posturing indicates severe intracranial pathology
  • Drug chart review: recent opioids, benzodiazepines, anticonvulsants, sedatives — all can cause or worsen reduced consciousness
  • Temperature: hypothermia (<35°C) causes altered consciousness — check even in warm environments
E — Exposure
  • Fully expose the patient with dignity maintained
  • Temperature: core temperature — fever (>38°C) or hypothermia (<35°C)
  • Skin: non-blanching petechiae/purpura (meningococcal — call for help immediately), urticaria/angioedema (anaphylaxis), surgical wounds, pressure sores
  • Abdomen: distension, rigidity, guarding, tenderness, bowel sounds
  • Limbs: pitting oedema (bilateral = systemic; unilateral = consider DVT), calf tenderness, erythema
  • Drains and lines: output volume and character, site infection, patency
  • Back: sacral oedema, wounds not visible from front, spinal tenderness
  • Fluid balance: review the chart — cumulative balance, last 4–6 hours of output

Hypoglycaemia

Glucose <4.0 mmol/L — act immediately
  • Conscious and able to swallow: GlucoGel oral, or 150–200ml of 10% glucose IV
  • Glucose <3.0 mmol/L or unconscious: 100ml of 20% glucose IV
  • No IV access: glucagon 1mg IM
  • Recheck glucose at 15 minutes. Recheck at 60 minutes.
  • Establish cause. If on insulin or sulphonylurea — review the regimen with the team.

⚠ Always follow your local trust hypoglycaemia protocol — concentrations and volumes may vary between trusts.

Your Own State

You will get tired. You will feel hungry and then stop feeling hungry. You will make decisions at 4am that you would not make at 10am.

Eat before midnight. Drink water throughout the shift. Sit down when you can. If you feel genuinely unsafe — too tired to think clearly — escalate this to your registrar or the site coordinator. This is patient safety, not weakness.

Call the registrar immediately — overnight
  • Suspected sepsis (NEWS2 ≥5 + suspected infection) — initiate Sepsis 6 simultaneously
  • Peri-arrest: HR >150 or <40, or BP <80 systolic with symptoms
  • Suspected acute MI: chest pain + ECG changes
  • Acute stroke: new focal neurology, FAST positive
  • GI bleed with haemodynamic compromise

Chapter 07

The Unwritten Rules

Ward culture, hierarchy, and the things no one in medical school told you.

No one tells you this in medical school. By the time you learn it, you've already made the mistakes.

The People Who Will Save You

Nurses

Ward nurses know their patients better than you do. They've been there every day. They've noticed the subtle changes. When a nurse says "I'm not happy with this patient", take it seriously — their track record is better than yours at this point. Do not dismiss a nursing concern because you cannot find a clinical explanation. Investigate it.

The relationship you build with nursing staff in the first month determines whether your on-calls are manageable or miserable.

Ward pharmacists

Will catch your prescribing errors before they reach the patient. If they query a drug, engage. They're doing their job.

Physio and OT

A patient who is medically ready but cannot manage stairs at home is not ready for discharge. Involve them early — not the day before.

HCAs, site coordinator, and everyone else

HCAs take most of the observations. They change dressings and notice things at 3am. The site coordinator knows where every patient in the hospital is and where beds are coming from. Treat everyone with the same professional respect you'd give a consultant.

Ward Round Culture

Arrive before the consultant. Know your patients. Have the observations, overnight events, and investigation results ready. Take notes. Write down what was decided for each patient — the ward round is the most information-dense part of the day and you will forget if you don't.

If you disagree with a decision on the ward round

Say so in the room. Not in the corridor, not to the nurse — in the room, professionally.

"I want to flag — the renal function this morning was worse than expected. Does that change the plan for the furosemide?"

You are part of the medical team. You are expected to contribute, not just to transcribe.

Hierarchy

The NHS has a clear hierarchy that exists for mostly sensible reasons. Within it, there is still room to advocate for your patients and flag clinical concerns. If a senior makes a decision you disagree with, raise it once, clearly. If they have heard your concern and still want to proceed, carry out the instruction — unless it is unsafe or unethical, in which case escalate further.

The hierarchy is not there to suppress clinical judgment. It is there to ensure complex decisions are made by experienced people with full information. Your job is to ensure they have full information.

Practical Specifics

Things that are non-negotiable
  • Do not carry patient drug charts off the ward
  • Do not discuss patients in the lift, canteen, or public spaces using names or identifiable details
  • Always date, sign, and add your GMC number to every note entry
  • If you make a prescribing error, document it accurately and complete a Datix incident report. This is not optional — it is how the NHS learns from mistakes and how you protect yourself
On Datix

Completing a Datix report is not an admission of guilt. It is not "reporting yourself." It is a mandatory part of clinical governance, and a Datix entry you write is far better than one written about you by someone else. If something happens to a patient and you're unsure how to document it — call your registrar or educational supervisor before writing anything.

The Things No One Says Aloud

  • "I don't know" is an acceptable clinical answer — if it is followed by "but I will find out."
  • Asking for help is a sign of clinical maturity, not incompetence.
  • The most dangerous doctor on the ward is the one who doesn't know what they don't know.
  • If you are worried about something and you cannot put it into clinical language, write down that you are worried anyway. Date it, sign it. It may matter later.

Chapter 08

Self-Care and Burnout

Recognising when you're running on empty and what to do about it.

Six months in. You've stopped going to the gym. You haven't called your parents in three weeks. You fall asleep on the commute home and feel anxious the whole way in. The job still feels just about manageable. That feeling is not a reliable indicator of how you are doing.

Burnout does not arrive suddenly. It is a gradual process of depletion — professional, emotional, and physical — that most junior doctors don't notice until they are deep in it.

What Burnout Looks Like

Early warning signs — watch for these specifically
  • Increasing cynicism about patients — thinking "not another one" about presentations that would previously have interested you
  • Going through the motions on a ward round without clinical curiosity
  • Making decisions faster than you should because you no longer have the energy to think carefully
  • Sleeping a lot and still feeling exhausted
  • Using alcohol more regularly as a way to decompress after shifts
  • Feeling that you are bad at your job, regardless of evidence to the contrary

These are not personality flaws. They are physiological and psychological responses to sustained high-demand work.

What Actually Helps

Sleep and food

Non-negotiable. Night shift recovery takes longer than it feels like it should — give it 48 hours. Bring food to night shifts. Eat before long days. This is infrastructure, not lifestyle advice.

Something that is not medicine

A hobby, a sport, a relationship, a creative interest — something that belongs entirely to you and has no professional consequence. Protect it actively.

Formal Support — Know Where to Go

Support options for NHS doctors
  • Occupational Health — You can self-refer. Conversations are confidential. Available at every NHS trust.
  • Practitioner Health (practitionerhealth.nhs.uk) — Specialist confidential service for doctors experiencing mental health problems, stress, or addiction.
  • BMA Counselling — 0330 123 1245. Free, confidential, available 24 hours.
  • Educational Supervisor — The right person to approach if you are struggling with a clinical situation, have experienced something distressing, or believe your workload is unsafe.

When to Step Back

If you are making clinical decisions that you believe are below your own standard because of your mental or physical state, you have a professional obligation to flag this. To your registrar, consultant, or site coordinator.

Ask yourself two questions:

The two questions
  • Would I want this doctor making decisions about me right now?
  • If something went wrong tonight and this came to a coroner's court, could I justify why I continued?

If the answer to either is no: stop, escalate, and get support.

A final note

Starting FY1 is genuinely hard. You will make mistakes — not because you are incompetent, but because medicine is complex and you are human. What matters is not the absence of error, but how you respond: openly, honestly, and with a commitment to learning.

The registrar who looks completely unfazed in the middle of a cardiac arrest was once exactly where you are now. They learned by doing, by asking, and by being honest about what they didn't know.

That is exactly what you are going to do.

Chapter 09

Portfolio, CBDs, and Your ARCP

Keeping up with your Foundation Programme requirements without letting them swamp you.

It's the end of your first rotation. Your educational supervisor meeting is in two weeks. You open your e-Portfolio and realise you haven't documented a single workplace-based assessment since you started. This is fixable — but only if you start now, not the day before the ARCP.

The Foundation Programme has a formal set of competency requirements you need to demonstrate across your two years of training. They are standardised across the UK curriculum and reviewed at your Annual Review of Competence Progression (ARCP). Failing to collect evidence is one of the most avoidable causes of an unsatisfactory ARCP outcome — and it almost always comes down to leaving everything to the last month.

Your e-Portfolio

Every foundation doctor in the UK has an e-Portfolio account through the UK Foundation Programme. This is the central record of your training — supervised learning events, reflections, feedback, supervisor reports, and sign-offs all live here.

Log in during the first week of your first post. Familiarise yourself with the structure before you need to submit anything. Your Educational Supervisor and Clinical Supervisor will both access it.

Key items you will need to collect
  • Case-Based Discussions (CBDs) — structured discussions with a supervisor about a clinical case you have managed. The conversation matters more than the form; use it as genuine reflective practice.
  • Mini-CEX (Mini Clinical Evaluation Exercise) — a direct observation of a clinical encounter: history-taking, examination, or a procedure. Your assessor rates specific competencies.
  • Multi-Source Feedback (MSF) — 360° feedback from colleagues including nurses, doctors, and allied health professionals. Initiated by you; completed anonymously.
  • Direct Observation of Procedural Skills (DOPS) — assessed observation of practical procedures: cannulation, ABG, catheterisation, venesection.
  • Reflections — written reflections on significant events, learning points, and personal development. These are not just tick-boxes — a well-written reflection demonstrates growth and is looked at carefully at ARCP.

Your ARCP

The Annual Review of Competence Progression is an annual panel review of your e-Portfolio evidence. The panel — typically including your Foundation Programme Director — determines whether you have met the curriculum requirements for the year. A satisfactory outcome means you progress. An unsatisfactory outcome can mean a requirement to repeat a post.

What the panel looks at
  • Completion of the required number and type of workplace-based assessments
  • Quality and depth of reflective practice
  • Educational and Clinical Supervisor reports
  • Evidence of meeting the required curriculum outcomes for FY1 and FY2
  • Attendance and engagement with teaching
  • Any concerns raised during the year

The System That Works

The doctors who sail through ARCP are not the ones who do more work — they are the ones who document consistently throughout the year rather than in a panic at the end. The requirements are not onerous if spread across the year.

  1. Book your Educational Supervisor meetings early. You are entitled to regular meetings. Use them. Bring cases, reflections, and specific questions. A supervisor who knows you will write a stronger report.
  2. Aim for at least one workplace-based assessment per fortnight. This is achievable during any clinical rotation. Ask a registrar or consultant at the end of a ward round or clinic — it takes fifteen minutes.
  3. Write reflections contemporaneously. A reflection written the same day as an event is specific, honest, and useful. A reflection written in month six about something that happened in month one is generic and unconvincing.
  4. Request your MSF early in each placement. Colleagues need time to complete it, and you need time to act on the feedback before your supervisor report is written.
  5. Check your curriculum outcomes mid-rotation. If there are specific competencies you haven't evidenced yet, make a plan before the last two weeks of the post.
CBDs — use them well

A CBD is not just an administrative requirement. It is a structured conversation with a senior colleague about a case you managed — what you did, why, what you would do differently, and what you have learned. The best CBDs happen naturally after a complex admission or an interesting ward round discussion. They do not need to be formal or time-consuming.

The cases worth documenting are the ones that made you think, the ones where something unexpected happened, or the ones where you felt uncertain and had to make a decision anyway. Those are the cases that demonstrate genuine clinical development.

What to do if you're falling behind

Tell your Educational Supervisor at the next meeting — not two weeks before ARCP. Most educational supervisors can help you develop a catch-up plan if there is time to implement it. Surprises at the ARCP panel are far harder to manage than deficits flagged early.

Escalate to your Educational Supervisor if
  • You are in a post where supervisors are consistently unavailable for assessments
  • You have received feedback that concerns you and you are unsure how to respond to it
  • You are approaching the end of a rotation with significant outstanding requirements
  • Any incident or concern has been raised about your practice that you believe should be addressed before ARCP

Risk stratification in community-acquired pneumonia.

Scoring — 1 point each
  • C — Confusion (new, AMT ≤8)
  • U — Urea >7 mmol/L
  • R — Respiratory rate ≥30/min

2

Moderate risk. Consider short admission or supervised outpatient treatment.

High risk. Hospital admission. Score ≥4: consider ICU assessment.

Important

CURB-65 is a guide, not a rule. A 45-year-old with a score of 1 but SpO₂ 86% is not suitable for outpatient treatment. Always integrate the score with clinical assessment.
