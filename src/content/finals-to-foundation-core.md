# Finals to Foundation: Core
### A survival guide for starting FY1

*For education and preparation only. Always follow your trust's protocols and escalate to a senior colleague when in doubt. Drug doses should be verified against the BNF and your local formulary before prescribing.*

---

## Before We Start

You have spent five years learning medicine. You know the pathophysiology of heart failure, the mechanism of metformin, the embryological basis of a patent ductus arteriosus. None of that is what this guide is about.

This guide is about the gap — the space between what you know and what you actually need to do at 7am on a ward with nineteen patients, a bleep that hasn't stopped since handover, and a nurse asking you to chart analgesia for a patient you've never met.

The gap is real, it is wide, and no one warned you about it. This guide does.

What follows is what a good registrar would tell you on your first day if they had the time — and none of them do.

---

## Chapter 1: Reading Guidelines

**It's 9am. A patient has a UTI. You think she needs nitrofurantoin but you're not sure about the dose, whether she's in the right age group, or what the local resistance pattern looks like. You have seven other jobs. You need an answer in under three minutes.**

Guidelines are not meant to be read start to finish. They are reference documents. Your job is to navigate them at speed, extract the relevant paragraph, and apply clinical judgment to what you find.

Here is how you do that.

### The Hierarchy

**Local trust protocol** overrides everything else for day-to-day prescribing decisions. Most trusts have antibiotic guidelines, VTE prophylaxis protocols, and insulin management pathways. Find yours in the first week — they are usually on the trust intranet or a dedicated app. This is the first thing you look up, not NICE.

**NICE CKS** (cks.nice.org.uk) is your next stop. These are short, structured summaries written for clinical use. Each condition has a "Prescribing information" or "Management" section. Go there directly. The "Background" and "Causes" sections are for revision, not for 9am on the ward.

**Full NICE guidelines** are for complex decisions, audit, or when CKS doesn't cover your specific question. They are long. Use the search function and go to the specific recommendation, not the executive summary.

**SIGN guidelines** are the Scottish equivalent. Useful, particularly in specialties where SIGN has produced stronger guidance than NICE. The format is similar — navigate to the recommendation table.

### How to Find the Answer Fast

1. Go to your local antibiotic/trust guideline first.
2. If it's not there, go to NICE CKS.
3. Search the specific condition or drug, not the broad topic.
4. Read the recommendation, not the evidence base.
5. Check the date — anything over three years old for an antibiotic guideline is worth cross-referencing.

### The Part No One Tells You

NICE guidelines describe what *should* happen in an ideal clinical environment. The ward is not an ideal clinical environment.

A guideline that says "consider referral to cardiology for all patients with new AF" does not mean you call the cardiology registrar at 2am for a patient who was in AF three years ago and is medically stable. Clinical judgment is applying the guideline to the patient in front of you, not treating the guideline as a checklist.

When you are unsure whether the guideline applies, ask: *is my patient in the population the guideline was written for?* If yes, follow it. If no — or if you genuinely can't tell — escalate.

**Red flags — escalate if:**
- Local and national guidelines directly contradict each other and you cannot determine which applies
- You are being asked to deviate from a guideline without a documented clinical reason
- The guideline is over five years old and you're making a decision where new evidence would materially change management

## Chapter 2: Prescribing Safely

**It's 11pm. The nurse bleeps you to say a patient is in pain and asks you to prescribe some analgesia. You pull up the drug chart. The patient is 78 years old, has CKD stage 3, and is already on codeine. You're tired. This feels simple. It isn't.**

Prescribing errors are the most common source of avoidable harm caused by junior doctors. Not because FY1s are careless — but because the system is complex, the information is scattered, and the workload is relentless.

You will not eliminate prescribing errors by being more careful. You will reduce them by having a system.

### Before You Prescribe Anything

Run this check every single time, even when it feels excessive:

**1. Check the allergy status.** Look at the drug chart and in the EPMA system. If there is an allergy documented and you are about to prescribe something in that class, stop. Check if it's a true allergy or an intolerance (if the notes say "rash" to amoxicillin, that warrants a different conversation than "anaphylaxis"). If unsure, ask pharmacy.

**2. Check the renal function.** Any patient with AKI, CKD stage 3+, or an unexpectedly high creatinine requires dose adjustment for renally-cleared drugs. The drugs that bite hardest here are: NSAIDs (avoid), gentamicin (requires levels), metformin (hold in AKI), direct oral anticoagulants (dose-adjust or avoid), and many antibiotics. When in doubt, check the BNF renal impairment guidance.

**3. Check for interactions.** EPMA systems will flag these, but they flag everything — including interactions that are clinically irrelevant. If you get a warning, read it. The ones that matter: warfarin + almost anything, lithium + NSAIDs/diuretics/ACEi, SSRIs + tramadol (serotonin syndrome risk), QTc-prolonging drugs together.

**4. Check the weight** for weight-based dosing (heparin, gentamicin, enoxaparin).

### The Drugs That Cause the Most Harm

**Opioids.** The most common FY1 opioid error is prescribing a dose without considering what the patient is already on. A patient on `morphine 30mg SR BD` who you give `oramorph 10mg` prn to overnight needs that dose reviewed. If a patient is opioid-naive, start low. `Oramorph 2.5–5mg` oral prn is appropriate for most adults. `10mg` in a frail elderly patient can be catastrophic.

Always prescribe an antiemetic alongside any new opioid initiation. Always prescribe a laxative alongside regular opioids.

**Insulin.** Never prescribe insulin without knowing the patient's current regimen, their most recent glucose, and why you are prescribing. Insulin sliding scales are managed by nursing staff — your job is to prescribe the correct type at the correct dose and ensure the parameters are appropriate. If you are unfamiliar with a patient's insulin regimen, call the diabetic specialist nurse before prescribing.

**Anticoagulants.** Check the indication, the duration, and whether bridging therapy is needed if a procedure is planned. If a patient is on warfarin, you need to know their current INR before prescribing anything that interacts. If you're starting a DOAC, confirm renal function and the appropriate dose.

**Antibiotics.** Follow local protocol. If empirical antibiotics have been started, they need review at 48–72 hours when cultures are back — this is your responsibility to flag on the drug chart and handover. Do not continue broad-spectrum antibiotics beyond that window without a clear reason documented.

**Paracetamol.** It feels safe. It is safe if dosed correctly.

- `Paracetamol 1g QDS` (max `4g/24h`) for adults ≥50kg
- Reduce to `Paracetamol 500mg QDS` (max `2g/24h`) if weight <50kg, or if the patient has hepatic impairment, is malnourished, or is a chronic alcohol user
- Check the patient isn't already getting paracetamol in a combination product (co-codamol, co-dydramol)

### EPMA Systems

Every trust will have an electronic prescribing system (EPMA). Learn yours in the first week — before you need it under pressure. The most common errors with EPMA are: prescribing the wrong route (IV instead of oral or vice versa), selecting the wrong frequency (BD instead of TDS), and not completing the "indication" field which your pharmacist needs to review the chart.

Pharmacy will review your charts. Let them. If they query a prescription, that is the system working. Do not be defensive — explain your reasoning and update the chart.

### When You Are Not Sure

**Ask pharmacy.** Clinical pharmacists on wards are one of the most underused resources in the hospital. They know the drug interactions, the dose adjustments, the local formulary, and the contraindications better than you do. This is not an insult — it is their job and they are good at it. Call them.

**Red flags — escalate if:**
- A patient has a suspected allergic reaction to a drug you or a colleague prescribed
- You have prescribed something and later discover a significant interaction you missed
- A patient's renal function has deteriorated significantly and they are on nephrotoxic drugs
- You are being asked to prescribe something outside your competence — insulin regimens, chemotherapy, complex anticoagulation — and you cannot reach your registrar

## Chapter 3: Escalating Correctly

**It's 3am. Your patient's NEWS2 has gone from 2 to 7 in four hours. You know this is wrong. You bleep the registrar. The registrar sounds annoyed, asks what you've done so far, and gives you the impression they think you're overreacting. You start to doubt yourself. Don't.**

Escalation is a clinical skill. It is not about confidence or seniority — it is about having a clear structure and the conviction to use it.

### When to Escalate

**Escalate early. Escalate based on trajectory, not just current state.**

A patient with a NEWS2 of 5 at 2am who was 1 at midnight is more concerning than a patient with a chronic NEWS2 of 5 who is at their baseline. The trend matters more than the number.

Mandatory escalation triggers:
- `NEWS2 ≥7` at any point
- Any single parameter scoring `3` (respiratory rate, SpO2, systolic BP, heart rate, consciousness, temperature)
- Uncontrolled pain that isn't responding to your management
- Acute change in GCS, new confusion in a previously orientated patient
- Urine output `<0.5ml/kg/hour` for 2+ hours in a catheterised patient
- Any clinical gut feeling that the patient is deteriorating and you cannot explain why

The last point matters. If something feels wrong and you cannot put your finger on it, escalate. Your instinct is data.

### How to Escalate: SBAR

SBAR is not a bureaucratic framework. It is the format that gets registrars out of bed and into the ward.

**S — Situation**
State who you are, where you are, and what is happening now.
*"I'm the FY1 on Nightingale ward. I'm calling about Mr Davies in bed 4 — he's a 67-year-old admitted yesterday with a COPD exacerbation. His NEWS2 has gone from 2 to 7 in the last four hours and he's increasingly breathless."*

**B — Background**
Give the relevant clinical context. Not the whole history — the information the registrar needs to understand the situation.
*"He's on current treatment of pred and nebs. Chest X-ray from admission showed right lower lobe consolidation. He's on aspirin and bisoprolol at baseline, no known allergies."*

**A — Assessment**
Tell them what you think is happening.
*"I'm concerned he's deteriorating despite initial treatment. His respiratory rate is 28, SpO2 is 88% on 4L. I think he may need escalation to CPAP or ITU review."*

**R — Recommendation**
Tell them what you need.
*"I'd like you to come and review him now. I've already ordered an ABG and increased his oxygen."*

Notice the last line. Do what you can before you call. Start oxygen, get IV access, take bloods, do an ECG if relevant. The registrar's first question will be "what have you done?" Have an answer.

### Dealing with Pushback

You will encounter registrars who are dismissive. Some are tired. Some have a different threshold. Some are wrong.

If a registrar gives you phone advice that you are not comfortable acting on, or declines to come to review a patient who you genuinely believe is sick, document the conversation in the notes:

*"Registrar Dr [Name] contacted at 03:15. Advised to continue current management and review in the morning. My ongoing concern is documented above. I will repeat NEWS2 in one hour."*

Then act on your clinical judgment. If the patient deteriorates further, call the registrar again. If you cannot reach your registrar or you believe the patient is in immediate danger, call the on-call consultant directly. That is not going over someone's head — it is your professional obligation.

You are legally responsible for the patients under your care during your shift. So is the registrar. Document everything.

**Red flags — escalate immediately:**
- NEWS2 ≥7 or any single parameter of 3
- Unresponsive patient
- Respiratory arrest or circulatory compromise
- Anaphylaxis
- Suspected sepsis (NEWS2 ≥5 + suspected infection) — initiate Sepsis 6 before calling
- New focal neurology or sudden-onset severe headache

## Chapter 4: Clerking Efficiently

**You have a new referral from ED. The patient is a 71-year-old with a two-day history of worsening shortness of breath. You have fifteen minutes before the ward round starts. The ED clerk says the notes are "in the system". You have never met this patient.**

Clerking is not about writing a comprehensive medical history. It is about building a clinical picture fast enough to make a safe decision.

### The Structure

Work through this in order. Each section has a purpose beyond documentation — it is generating your differential and ruling out red flags in real time.

**Presenting complaint (PC)**
One line. What brought them in today, in their words.

**History of presenting complaint (HPC)**
Drill into the presenting symptom: onset, duration, character, radiation, severity (1–10), associated symptoms, exacerbating/relieving factors, previous episodes. For respiratory: cough, sputum, haemoptysis, wheeze, pleuritic pain, orthopnoea, PND. For cardiac: chest pain, palpitations, syncope, ankle swelling. For abdominal: site, radiation, bowel habit, PR bleeding, nausea/vomiting, appetite, weight loss.

**Past medical history (PMH)**
Active conditions that affect management. Specifically: heart disease (IHD, AF, CCF), diabetes, hypertension, renal impairment, lung disease (COPD, asthma, bronchiectasis), liver disease, previous DVT/PE, malignancy. For any surgical history — which operation, when, any complications.

**Drug history and allergies (DHx)**
Write every drug with dose and frequency. Use the GP letter or EPMA — do not rely on patient recall for dose information. Allergies: document what the reaction was, not just the drug.

**Social history (SHx)**
Smoking (pack years = packs/day × years), alcohol (units/week), occupational history if relevant. Living situation: own home, care home, who else is in the house, functional baseline (can they walk to the shops? manage stairs? cook for themselves?). This section determines your discharge planning from the moment you clerk them.

**Family history (FHx)**
First-degree relatives with IHD, malignancy, or heritable conditions relevant to the presentation. Not always necessary — use judgment.

**Review of systems (ROS)**
A targeted screen for things the patient hasn't mentioned. Focus on red flag symptoms: unintentional weight loss, haemoptysis, PR bleeding, haematuria, neurological symptoms, night sweats.

**Examination**
General: alert/confused, well/unwell, comfortable/distressed. Hands (clubbing, peripheral stigmata), face (pallor, jaundice, cyanosis), JVP. Then system-specific. Document vitals and NEWS2 score. Document the findings, not the absence of findings — "heart sounds I+II+0, no murmurs" not "cardiovascular examination unremarkable."

**Impression and plan**
This is the most important part of the clerking. Write your differential in order of probability, not severity. Then document your immediate management plan: investigations requested, treatment started, what you are waiting for, and what would change your management.

### Speed Without Missing Red Flags

The most dangerous clerking errors are not in what you document incorrectly — they are in what you fail to notice.

**The questions that flag the emergencies:**
- "Have you ever had a clot in your leg or lung?" → DVT/PE history in a dyspnoeic patient
- "Have you coughed up any blood?" → malignancy, PE, bronchiectasis
- "Have you lost any weight without trying?" → malignancy until proven otherwise
- "Do you use any recreational drugs?" → essential in unexplained cardiac presentations in under-50s
- "Has anyone ever told you that your heart is abnormal?" → undiagnosed structural heart disease

Ask about the medication list specifically for immunosuppressants, long-term steroids, anticoagulants, and methotrexate — all of which change your entire management if missed.

**Red flags — escalate before completing the clerking if:**
- Patient becomes acutely unwell during the history
- NEWS2 ≥5 on initial observations
- You find signs of sepsis, haemorrhage, or airway compromise
- The history suggests an acute surgical abdomen, aortic dissection, or subarachnoid haemorrhage

## Chapter 5: Handover That Works

**It's 17:15. You have nine outstanding jobs, two unwell patients, and a night team who you have never met arriving in forty-five minutes. The quality of your handover will determine whether your patients are safe overnight.**

Bad handover kills patients. Not dramatically, not usually — but quietly, through missed jobs, lost information, and a night team left uncertain about what to watch for.

Good handover is not long. It is specific.

### The Framework

Use SBAR for each patient you hand over verbally. For a written handover list, use this structure per patient:

**Name, age, bed, admission diagnosis**
**Active issues right now** — what is happening, what you have done, and what is expected
**Outstanding jobs** — bloods, imaging, referrals, drug charts to complete
**If-then instructions** — "if NEWS2 rises above 5, call the registrar; she's aware"
**What normal looks like for this patient** — essential for patients with complex baselines

### What to Hand Over

**Hand over the sick patients first.** Not in bed order, not alphabetically — in order of clinical concern. The night team needs to know immediately who to worry about.

**Hand over the jobs that cannot wait** until morning. Antibiotics that need reviewing, pain that is not controlled, results that need acting on. If a job can wait until the day team, say so explicitly — that is useful information.

**Hand over uncertainty.** If you think a patient might deteriorate and you're not sure why, say that. *"I've not been happy with Mrs Khan all afternoon. I can't find a clear reason. Please keep an eye on her"* is one of the most important things you can say at handover.

### What Not to Hand Over

Do not hand over jobs that you can do in the next forty-five minutes. Signing a discharge letter, completing a drug chart, chasing a result you're about to get — do them. The night team inherits your incomplete list, and unlike you, they are covering the whole hospital.

Do not give the night team a differential diagnosis. Give them the working diagnosis and the management plan. If the diagnosis is genuinely uncertain, hand over the uncertainty — not a list of twelve possible causes.

### Verbal Handover

When handing over verbally, ask the night doctor to read back any specific if-then instructions. Not because they are incompetent — because verbal information is unreliable and this is how you confirm it was received.

Write the key instructions in the notes for any patient you are genuinely worried about. *"FY1 to night team: please review if NEWS2 ≥5. Registrar Dr [name] is aware and has asked to be called directly."* This protects the patient and protects you.

**Red flags — do not leave the ward until:**
- Every acutely unwell patient has been verbally handed over to the night team directly
- The night registrar knows about any patient who may need ITU review
- Outstanding urgent jobs have been communicated and confirmed received

## Chapter 6: Nights and On-Calls

**It's 2:47am. You have eleven tasks on your list. Your bleep goes off. A patient on the surgical ward has a temperature of 38.4°C and the nurse wants you to prescribe antibiotics. You haven't eaten since 6pm. You've been on since 9am yesterday.**

Nights are survivable. Not comfortable — survivable. The difference between a good on-call and a dangerous one is not talent or experience. It is prioritisation.

### Prioritising Your Bleep List

When bleeps come in faster than you can respond, you need a triage system. Use this:

**Category 1 — go now, everything else waits:**
- Cardiac or respiratory arrest (you should be running)
- Unresponsive patient
- Acute haemorrhage
- Anaphylaxis
- Patient with NEWS2 ≥7 or rapidly deteriorating

**Category 2 — within 20 minutes:**
- NEWS2 4–6 in a patient not previously reviewed
- Hypoglycaemia (`glucose <4.0 mmol/L` in a symptomatic patient)
- Acute urinary retention
- Severe uncontrolled pain
- Acute chest pain

**Category 3 — within the hour:**
- Drug chart queries, routine prescriptions
- Results that need reviewing but are not acutely abnormal
- Catheter bypassing or blocking without systemic signs
- Sleep sedation requests

**Category 4 — can wait until morning:**
- Non-urgent discharge letters
- Routine TTOs
- Stable patients with minor queries

When you get a bleep, ask the nurse: "What is their NEWS2?" This tells you immediately which category you are dealing with. Any nurse who cannot tell you should go and check before you decide whether to drop everything.

### The Sick Patient at Night

You will be called about patients you have never met, on wards you have never been on, with notes you cannot easily access. This is normal. This is nights.

When you arrive at a sick patient:

1. **Look at the patient first.** Before you look at the chart, before you ask the nurse anything. Do they look well or unwell? Are they conscious? Are they breathing? Your eyes are faster than any investigation.

2. **Get the observations.** HR, BP, RR, SpO2, temperature, GCS. Calculate NEWS2. If you didn't calculate it before leaving the nurses' station, do it now.

3. **Get immediate access.** Can you talk to this patient? Are they oriented? If not, you need IV access and blood cultures before you do anything else.

4. **Do the ABCDE assessment:**
   - **A**irway: Is it patent? Can they speak?
   - **B**reathing: Rate, depth, work of breathing, SpO2, air entry, percussion
   - **C**irculation: HR, BP, cap refill, JVP, skin temperature and colour
   - **D**isability: GCS, pupils, glucose (always check glucose in any acutely unwell patient)
   - **E**xposure: Temperature, any obvious source of bleeding, rashes, wounds

5. **Treat as you go.** Oxygen if SpO2 <94% (or <88% in known COPD). IV access and fluids if haemodynamically compromised. `500ml sodium chloride 0.9%` over 15 minutes if the patient is clearly in shock. Do not wait until you have a diagnosis to start treatment.

6. **Escalate.** If you have treated for 15 minutes and the patient is not improving, call the registrar. You do not need to have a diagnosis to escalate. "I've started treatment but they're not responding and I'm worried" is sufficient.

### Glucose

Check glucose on every acutely unwell patient who hasn't had one in the last two hours. Hypoglycaemia is immediately reversible and immediately fatal if missed.

- `Glucose <4.0 mmol/L` in a conscious, symptomatic patient: `150–200ml` of `10% glucose` IV, or `Glucogel` orally if they can swallow
- `Glucose <3.0 mmol/L` or unconscious: `100ml of 20% glucose` IV (or `glucagon 1mg IM` if no IV access)
- Recheck glucose 15 minutes after treatment

### Your Own State

You will get tired. You will feel hungry and then stop feeling hungry. You will make decisions at 4am that you would not make at 10am. You know this intellectually, but you won't fully believe it until it happens.

Eat before midnight. Drink water throughout the shift. Sit down when you can.

If you feel unsafe — genuinely unsafe, unable to think clearly, so tired you are making errors — you are legally and professionally required to escalate this to your registrar or the site coordinator. This is not weakness. This is patient safety.

**Red flags — call the registrar immediately:**
- Any patient with suspected sepsis (NEWS2 ≥5 + suspected infection source) — initiate Sepsis 6 simultaneously
- Cardiac arrest or peri-arrest (HR >150 or <40, BP <80 systolic with symptoms)
- Suspected acute MI (chest pain + ECG changes)
- Acute stroke symptoms (new focal neurology, FAST positive)
- GI bleed with haemodynamic compromise

## Chapter 7: The Unwritten Rules

**No one tells you this in medical school. By the time you learn it, you've already made the mistakes.**

The ward has a social structure that is nowhere in any curriculum. Understanding it does not make you a political animal — it makes you functional.

### The People Who Will Save You

**Nurses.** The ward nurses know their patients better than you do. They have been there every day. They have noticed the subtle changes. When a nurse says "I'm not happy with this patient", take it seriously — their track record is better than yours. The relationship you build with nursing staff in the first month determines whether your on-calls are manageable or miserable.

Do not dismiss a nurse's concern because you cannot find a clinical explanation. Investigate it. If you still can't find anything, document that you reviewed the patient and the nurses' concern. Come back in an hour.

Thank nurses when they help you. This is not performative — it is factual acknowledgement of work done.

**Ward pharmacists.** Already covered in prescribing, but worth repeating: they will catch your errors before they reach the patient. Work with them. If they query a drug, engage with the query. They are not criticising your prescribing — they are doing their job.

**HCAs (Healthcare Assistants).** They take most of the observations. They change dressings, mobilise patients, and notice things at 3am when no one else is around. Treat them with the same professional respect you would treat a registrar. This should not need saying.

**Physiotherapists and Occupational Therapists.** Invaluable for discharge planning. A patient who clinically is ready for discharge but cannot get up the stairs at home is not ready for discharge. Get the physio and OT involved early — waiting until the day before discharge is how patients get stuck.

**The site coordinator (bed manager).** They know where every patient in the hospital is, where beds are coming from, and what is happening at the front door. In a capacity crisis, they are your ally. Be straightforward with them and they will be straightforward with you.

### Ward Round Culture

Arrive before the consultant. Know your patients. If you're presenting — and as FY1 you will be — have the observations, the overnight events, and the investigation results ready. "I don't know" is acceptable if followed by "but I'll find out before we reach the bedside."

Take notes on the ward round. This is not clerical work — it is how you know what was decided for each patient and avoid errors in the post-round job list. The ward round is the most information-dense part of the day. Write it down.

If a plan is made on the ward round that you believe is incorrect, or you have information that changes the picture, say so in the room. Not in the corridor afterwards. Not to the nurse. In the room, clearly and professionally. *"I want to flag — the renal function this morning was worse than expected. Does that change the plan for the furosemide?"*

You are part of the medical team. You are expected to contribute, not just to transcribe.

### Hierarchy

The NHS has a clear hierarchy. It exists for reasons that are mostly sensible. Within that hierarchy, there is still room to advocate for your patients and to flag clinical concerns.

If a senior makes a decision you disagree with, raise it once, clearly. If they have heard your concern and still want to proceed, document your concern in the notes and carry out the instruction — unless it is unsafe, unethical, or you believe it will directly harm the patient, in which case escalate further up the chain.

The hierarchy is not there to suppress clinical judgment. It is there to ensure complex decisions are made by experienced people with full information. Your job is to make sure they have full information.

### Some Practical Specifics

- Do not carry patients' drug charts out of the ward. This sounds obvious. It happens.
- Do not discuss patients in the lift, in the canteen, or in a public space with names or identifiable details.
- Do not write in notes without dating, signing, and adding your GMC number.
- If you make an error, document it accurately in the notes and complete a Datix incident report. This is not optional. It is how the NHS learns from mistakes. It is also how you protect yourself from allegations of concealment.
- If something happens to a patient and you are unsure how to document it, call your registrar or educational supervisor. Do not attempt to manage it alone.

## Chapter 8: Self-Care and Burnout

**Six months in. You've stopped going to the gym. You haven't called your parents in three weeks. You fall asleep on the commute home and feel anxious the whole way in. The job still feels just about manageable. That feeling is not a reliable indicator of how you are doing.**

Burnout does not arrive suddenly. It is a gradual process of depletion — professional, emotional, and physical — that most junior doctors don't notice until they are deep in it.

This chapter will not solve burnout. But it will tell you what it looks like early, and what to do about it.

### What Burnout Looks Like

Early signs are specific, not generic. Watch for:
- Increasing cynicism about patients — thinking "not another one" about presentations that would previously have interested you
- Going through the motions on a ward round without clinical curiosity
- Making decisions faster than you should because you no longer have the energy to think carefully
- Avoiding tasks you used to find straightforward
- Sleeping a lot and still feeling exhausted
- Using alcohol more regularly as a way to decompress after shifts
- Feeling that you are bad at your job, regardless of evidence to the contrary

These are not personality flaws. They are physiological and psychological responses to sustained high-demand work.

### What Actually Helps

**Sleep.** Non-negotiable. A tired brain does not get better with willpower. Night shift recovery takes longer than it feels like it should. Give it 48 hours before concluding you're permanently broken.

**Eating properly.** Not because it's virtuous, but because your brain runs on glucose and you need to make safe clinical decisions. Bring food to night shifts. Eat breakfast before long days. This is infrastructure, not lifestyle advice.

**Something that is not medicine.** At the risk of stating the obvious: your identity is not your job. A hobby, a sport, a relationship, a creative interest — something that belongs entirely to you and has no professional consequence. Protect it.

**Talking to someone.** Whether that is a friend from medical school, a partner, a GP, or a consultant psychiatrist — talking about how you are doing is not an admission of failure. It is a protective behaviour.

### Formal Support

Every NHS trust has a **Consultant Occupational Health physician**. You can self-refer. Conversations are confidential. If you are struggling with your mental health, substance use, or a personal situation that is affecting your ability to work safely, Occupational Health exists for this reason.

Your **Educational Supervisor** should meet with you at least once per rotation. This is the right person to tell if you are struggling with a specific clinical situation, if you have experienced something distressing at work, or if you believe your workload is unsafe.

**Practitioner Health** (practitionerhealth.nhs.uk) is a confidential NHS service specifically for doctors and dentists. If you are experiencing mental health problems, addiction, or stress, this is a specialist service that understands the specific pressures of medical practice.

**The BMA's 24-hour counselling service** (0330 123 1245) is free, confidential, and available at any time.

### When to Step Back

If you are making clinical decisions that you believe are below your own standard because of your mental or physical state, you have a professional obligation to flag this. To your registrar, your consultant, or the site coordinator.

This is the most difficult judgment call in medicine — harder, in many ways, than a clinical emergency — because the pressure to keep going is immense and the personal cost of stepping back feels enormous.

The two questions to ask yourself:
1. Would I want this doctor making decisions about me right now?
2. If something went wrong tonight and this came to a coroner's court, would I be able to justify why I continued?

If the answer to either question is no: stop, escalate, and get support.

## Quick Reference: NEWS2

The National Early Warning Score 2. Used across NHS trusts to standardise the identification of deteriorating patients.

| SpO2 (Scale 1) | ≤91% | 92–93% | 94–95% | ≥96% | — | — | — |

| Heart rate | ≤40 | — | 41–50 | 51–90 | 91–110 | 111–130 | ≥131 |
| Consciousness | — | — | — | Alert | — | — | CVPU* |
| Temperature | ≤35.0 | — | 35.1–36.0 | 36.1–38.0 | 38.1–39.0 | ≥39.1 | — |

*CVPU: Confusion, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive

**Total NEWS2 score — response:**
- `0` — Minimum 12-hourly monitoring
- `1–4` — Minimum 4–6 hourly monitoring
- `5–6` OR single parameter `3` — Urgent review by ward-based clinician. Consider critical care review
- `≥7` — Emergency response. Immediate critical care/outreach review

Use **Scale 2** for SpO2 only in patients with confirmed hypercapnic respiratory failure (COPD, neuromuscular disease). Use Scale 1 for everyone else, including COPD patients who are not known to be hypercapnic.

## Quick Reference: SBAR

Use for every clinical escalation call, regardless of urgency.

**S — Situation**
Who you are, where you are calling from, which patient, and what is happening right now.
*"I'm Dr [Name], FY1 on Ward 7. I'm calling about Mr [Patient], 63 years old, bed 12. He's become increasingly confused over the last two hours and his NEWS2 is now 6."*

**B — Background**
The relevant clinical context — admission diagnosis, key history, current treatment, relevant investigations.
*"He was admitted two days ago with a right lower lobe pneumonia. He's on IV co-amoxiclav. Chest X-ray yesterday showed slight improvement."*

**A — Assessment**
Your clinical impression of what is happening.
*"I'm concerned he may be developing sepsis. His temperature is 39.2°C, HR 118, BP is 92/60. He's not responding well to the fluids I've started."*

**R — Recommendation**
What you need from the person you are calling.
*"I'd like you to come and review him now. I've taken blood cultures, started a fluid bolus, and I'm about to order a repeat CXR and ABG."*

## Quick Reference: CURB-65

Used for risk stratification in community-acquired pneumonia. Guides admission decisions.

**Scoring:**
- **C** — Confusion (new, AMT ≤8 or new disorientation)
- **U** — Urea >7 mmol/L
- **R** — Respiratory rate ≥30/min
- **B** — BP: systolic <90 mmHg OR diastolic ≤60 mmHg
- **65** — Age ≥65 years

Each criterion scores 1 point. Maximum score: 5.

**Interpretation:**
- `Score 0–1` — Low risk. Consider outpatient treatment
- `Score 2` — Moderate risk. Consider short-stay admission or supervised outpatient treatment
- `Score 3–5` — High risk. Hospital admission. Score ≥4 consider ICU assessment

**Important:** CURB-65 is a guide, not a rule. A 45-year-old with a score of 1 but a SpO2 of 86% is not suitable for outpatient treatment. Always integrate the score with clinical assessment.

## Quick Reference: Blood Reference Ranges

Standard adult reference ranges. Your trust may have slightly different values — always check against your local laboratory report.

**Haematology**
- Haemoglobin: `130–175 g/L` (male) / `115–160 g/L` (female)
- Haematocrit: `0.40–0.52` (male) / `0.36–0.48` (female)
- White cell count: `4.0–11.0 × 10⁹/L`
- Neutrophils: `1.8–7.5 × 10⁹/L`
- Lymphocytes: `1.0–4.0 × 10⁹/L`
- Platelets: `150–400 × 10⁹/L`
- MCV: `80–100 fL`
- Reticulocytes: `0.5–2.5%`

**Coagulation**
- PT (prothrombin time): `10–14 seconds`
- APTT: `25–35 seconds`
- INR (therapeutic for AF/VTE): `2.0–3.0`
- Fibrinogen: `1.5–4.0 g/L`

**Biochemistry**
- Sodium: `135–145 mmol/L`
- Potassium: `3.5–5.0 mmol/L`
- Urea: `2.5–7.8 mmol/L`
- Creatinine: `62–106 μmol/L` (male) / `44–80 μmol/L` (female)
- eGFR: `≥60 mL/min/1.73m²` (flag if <60)
- Glucose (fasting): `3.9–5.6 mmol/L`
- HbA1c: `<42 mmol/mol` (non-diabetic)
- Calcium (adjusted): `2.20–2.60 mmol/L`
- Magnesium: `0.7–1.0 mmol/L`
- Phosphate: `0.8–1.4 mmol/L`
- Bilirubin (total): `<21 μmol/L`
- ALT: `<40 U/L`
- AST: `<40 U/L`
- ALP: `30–130 U/L`
- GGT: `<60 U/L` (male) / `<40 U/L` (female)
- Albumin: `35–50 g/L`
- Total protein: `60–80 g/L`
- CRP: `<5 mg/L`

**Thyroid**
- TSH: `0.4–4.0 mU/L`
- Free T4: `10–22 pmol/L`

**Cardiac**
- Troponin I: trust-specific (high-sensitivity assays vary by manufacturer — check your local lab report)
- BNP: `<35 ng/L` (NT-proBNP: `<125 ng/L`)

**Arterial Blood Gas (breathing room air)**
- pH: `7.35–7.45`
- PaO2: `10.0–13.3 kPa`
- PaCO2: `4.7–6.0 kPa`
- HCO3: `22–26 mmol/L`
- Base excess: `−2 to +2`
- Lactate: `<2.0 mmol/L`

## A Final Note

Starting FY1 is genuinely hard. The hours are long, the stakes are real, and the transition from student to doctor is one of the most disorienting professional experiences most people ever have.

You will make mistakes. Not because you are incompetent — but because medicine is complex, the system is imperfect, and you are human. What matters is not the absence of error, but how you respond: openly, honestly, with a commitment to learning.

The registrar who seems completely unfazed in the middle of a cardiac arrest was once where you are now. They learned by doing, by asking, by getting things wrong in recoverable situations, and by being honest about what they didn't know.

That is exactly what you are going to do.

*Good luck.*
